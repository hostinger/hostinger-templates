The side street begins where efficiency ends. Turn left before the pharmacy and the station takes four minutes longer. There is no landmark, no view worth recommending, no secret café waiting to justify the detour. There are only houses, bins, a low brick wall, and the unremarkable evidence of lives carried on near one another.

For several months I walked this way home on Thursdays. At first it was an accident caused by roadworks. Later, when the barriers disappeared, habit preserved the diversion. The street became a narrow weekly calendar. I learned which garden produced the first snowdrops, which upstairs window turned blue at dusk, and which gate needed lifting before it would close.

## The argument against arrival

Main roads explain themselves. Their signs face strangers. Shops announce what happens inside; crossings instruct the body; numbered buses promise an elsewhere. On a side street, meaning is not arranged for passage. You receive it sideways.

A wooden chair appeared outside number fourteen with a paper sign: **TAKE ME**. It remained through two days of rain, the message becoming more urgent as the seat darkened. On the third Thursday it was gone, leaving four clean squares on the pavement where its legs had stood. I never knew whether this counted as rescue.

Farther along, chalk marks multiplied on a wall at child height. First a sun, then five uneven boxes, then the long blue body of an animal with wheels. Weather erased each drawing from the top down. The wall became a collaboration between invention and rain.

These were not events. That was their value. They asked for no response and offered no conclusion.

> Attention does not make the ordinary extraordinary. It lets the ordinary remain particular.

The distinction matters. To call every small thing magical is another way of refusing to see it. The gate is not enchanted. It sags because its lower hinge is loose. The snowdrops are common. The blue window is probably a television. Close looking should not turn a street into a stage set; it should return weight and cause to what is already there.

## Local knowledge, lightly held

After enough Thursdays, I began to recognise people without knowing them. The man with the folding bicycle left as I returned. A woman in a green coat carried two paper bags from the same grocer. An elderly terrier inspected one drain with professional consistency.

This knowledge felt both intimate and incomplete. I knew rhythms, not stories. It would have been easy to invent explanations and mistake them for sympathy. Better to keep the edges visible. A personal geography can include blank spaces.

One evening an ambulance blocked the street. Its lights turned every window alternately red and dark. Neighbours stood in doorways, careful not to gather too close. I passed quietly and did not write down the house number. Observation has limits. A notebook is not permission.

The following week the street looked unchanged, which did not mean nothing had changed.

## Four minutes

We often defend walking in the language of outcomes: steps counted, carbon avoided, mood improved. The side street gave me no measurable advantage. It was simply slower and, because it was slower, available in more detail.

Near the end of the route, the low wall held the day’s last warmth. In summer, sparrows dusted themselves beneath it. In autumn, crushed rowan berries made small orange maps on the pavement. Winter reduced the scene to brick, lichen, and the black joints of bare branches. Repetition did not exhaust the street. It provided the baseline against which change could appear.

I still take the main road when I am late. Its directness is a kindness. But on Thursdays, if weather and time allow, I turn before the pharmacy. The choice is too minor to call a ritual and too repeated to call chance.

Four minutes is not much to give a place. It is enough for the gate to lift, the blue window to brighten, and the street to become itself rather than the distance between two useful points.
