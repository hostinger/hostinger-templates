The river had already gone down by the time I arrived, but it had left its sentence in the branches. Straw, silver wrappers, and a single red glove hung shoulder-high in the willows. A brown rule crossed every trunk at the same impossible level. The path was open again. Mud held the latest weather more faithfully than memory did.

I came with the vague plan of measuring the flood. This is the sort of plan that makes a walk feel useful before it has begun. In my pocket were a pencil, a folded survey map, and a short ruler that could tell me almost nothing about a river. The official gauge stood two miles upstream, transmitting exact figures. Still, I wanted the local evidence: the snagged grass, the softened bank, the place where water had shouldered through last summer’s nettles.

## What remains

At the first bend, a bench faced the current. Someone had scraped the wet leaves from one half of it. I sat on the other half and watched pale foam revolve in an eddy. From there, the flood line was less a line than a loose collection of coincidences. A twig lodged here, a plastic lid there. Each object alone looked accidental. Together they became a contour.

We trust a mark because it repeats.

The same is true in a notebook. One observation is a flourish; three may be a pattern. I wrote down the height of the debris against five trees. I drew the angle of a fence that had begun to lean toward the sea. My figures were rough and my drawing worse, yet the act of recording slowed the scene enough for its smaller movements to enter: a wren working the exposed roots, a bottle turning green under water, rain dimpling only the sheltered half of the channel.

> A measurement can be a form of attention even when it proves nothing.

Near the footbridge, the path disappeared under a shallow brown sheet. Two cyclists carried their bicycles through the grass rather than wet their shoes. I followed their flattened route, then stopped where the bank had fallen away. The fresh edge exposed a dark seam of old leaves compressed beneath the topsoil. Yesterday’s ground had become today’s cross-section.

## A temporary archive

Floods rearrange the archive. They take a branch from one parish and set it down in another. They lift the private contents of a shed and distribute them without labels. They cover familiar paths, then return them changed by a few inches: enough to catch a toe, enough to redirect next season’s rain.

This one had not been disastrous here. The cottages were uphill and the road remained passable. That mattered. It let me look closely without confusing aesthetic interest for other people’s emergency. Downstream, fields had stayed submerged for days. A beautiful flood line can still be evidence of damage.

I put the ruler away. The useful unit was not inches but relation: water to bench, water to branch, water to the underside of the bridge. On the map I drew one blue pencil stroke beside the path. It was far too neat. Real water had entered every hollow and doubled back around every obstacle.

By noon, sunlight reached the opposite bank. The mud changed from brown to a brief metallic blue. Walkers began to appear, stepping around puddles with the confidence of people who had not seen the river at its highest. Soon the remaining debris would be cleared. Rain would soften the line on the trunks. New grass would hide the torn bank.

Before leaving, I returned to the bench. The cleared half had dried. On its back rail, just above my shoulder, a thin braid of grass marked where the river had been. I made one last note, not a number this time: *water sat here*. Then I closed the book and walked home above its sentence.
