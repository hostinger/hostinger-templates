At six in the morning, the bridge belonged to delivery vans and gulls. The lamps were still on, each making a weak amber coin on the pavement. Below, the river moved without reflecting much of anything. I leaned against the cold parapet and heard the first train before I saw its lit windows crossing behind the warehouses.

I had decided to visit the same place four times in one day. The method was borrowed from painters who return to a haystack or cathedral, though my equipment was only a notebook and a thermos. I wanted to see whether a fixed viewpoint could show time more clearly than a journey did.

## Morning: the working crossing

By eight, bicycles arrived in loose intervals. Their freewheels made a dry insect sound on the descent. School uniforms moved west; fluorescent jackets moved east. Nearly everyone crossed without looking at the water. This was not indifference. A bridge is most useful when it disappears into the errand.

The bridge itself recorded the traffic in small abrasions. Paint had worn from the rail where hands took the corner. A granite bollard held a crescent polished by bicycle pedals. On the northern pavement, one paving stone rocked under each passing wheel and returned with a soft click. These marks were a clock face made by repetition.

At nine, the church bell struck once, late enough that I checked my watch. A woman beside me checked hers too. We smiled at the shared correction.

## Noon: shadows become architecture

I returned when the bridge’s shadow had drawn itself almost directly beneath the arches. The river, brown in the morning, now carried hard pieces of sky. A pair of maintenance workers ate sandwiches in the recess above the central pier. One pointed at something downstream. I looked, but by then it had passed.

Noon compressed the view. Without long shadows, distances seemed smaller. The opposite bank moved closer; the warehouses flattened into painted scenery. Heat gathered in the dark metal handrail. I could keep my palm there for only a moment.

> A familiar place does not repeat itself. We repeat our route through it.

I wrote down the sounds in order: bus brake, pigeon wings, bottle in current, two notes from a radio, a child asking why the water was going backwards. The tide was rising. Around the pier, the upstream flow folded into a local contradiction and carried a twig briefly against the river.

## Evening: everyone pauses

At five thirty, stopping became acceptable. Office workers stood aside to finish calls. Two teenagers shared chips on the steps. A runner stretched with one foot against the parapet. The bridge, all function in the morning, became a room at the edge of the working day.

Low sun entered beneath the western arch and turned every ripple into wire. People noticed. Several lifted their phones. One man simply set down his shopping and watched until the light moved on.

This hour supplied the image I had expected to find: bridge, river, sunset, witness. It was beautiful and immediately suspicious. Had I visited only then, I would have mistaken the bridge for a viewing platform. The other hours corrected it. The place was also shortcut, shelter, meeting point, bottleneck, lunch table, weather instrument.

## Night: the lamps return

My final visit came after ten. Rain had started. The amber lamps recovered their circles on the pavement, brighter now on wet stone. Few people crossed. Each approach announced itself from far away: heels, tyres, the small rush of a hood being raised.

I stood where I had stood at dawn. Across the river, one warehouse window remained lit. The tide had covered the mud and climbed the lower steps. Nothing on the bridge displayed the day just passed, yet its surfaces held the accumulation of many such days.

Before I left, another train crossed beyond the roofs. For a moment, its windows aligned with the lamps along the bridge. Two rows of light moved at different speeds, one fixed and one travelling. Then the train was gone.

The bridge did not keep time like a clock. It kept time like a doorstep does: by being crossed, worn, left, and found again.
