# One

> Undercleeve, par. and vill.: 214 souls; one church (St Brannoc); one inn; one school; post office and forge. The valley is deep and keeps its own weather.
>
> — *County gazetteer, 1911*

The call came a little after seven, while I was lifting the cover from a drowned atlas.

I had the studio to myself at that hour, which is the way I prefer it. Conservation is slow work and it resents company. The atlas had come in from an estate sale in a crate of things nobody had wanted enough to dry — a century of a family’s paper, left one winter in a cellar that turned out to sit below the water table. I had the boards off and the text block wrapped in blotting paper, and I was easing the marbled cover away with a spatula the width of a butter knife when the phone went, and went, and went, because Aunt Tam does not believe in ringing off. She believes a telephone is a doorbell and that I am always home.

“You’ll want to sit down,” she said, instead of hello.

“I’m holding a hundred and forty years of the River Wye,” I said. “It can’t sit down and neither can I.”

“Nell.” Only my aunt can make one syllable sound like a hand laid flat on a table. “It’s out. The water’s gone down so far you can see the weathervane. Harry drove up Sunday and cried in the car park, and Harry didn’t cry when the dog died. They’re opening the shore to the families on Saturday. One week, they’re saying. Then the rains come back and take it under again.”

She did not say what “it” was. In our family there has only ever been one it.

I looked down at the atlas, at the county I’d uncovered an hour before — sheet forty-one, the old survey, the valley drawn in hachures fine as eyelashes, and in the crook of the river, in type so small it might have been a watermark, the name. Undercleeve. The mapmakers had died before the engineers were born, and none of them had known they were drawing the bottom of a lake.

“Nell,” Tam said, gentler now, the way you speak into a dark room when you’re not sure who’s in it. “It’s the first low water since your gran went. Somebody from the house ought to stand on that shore. And you’re the one she left the box to.”

***

Undercleeve went under on the last day of October, 1963.

The city was seventy miles off and thirsty, and the valley was deep and narrow-mouthed and inconveniently full of people. The engineers called it an ideal catchment. For six years there were letters and surveys and meetings in the school hall where men in good coats explained the future with a pointer, and for six years the village wrote back in careful ink and was thanked for its correspondence. Then one spring the letters stopped asking and started instructing, and the dam went up at the valley’s mouth like a hand closing over a jar.

My grandmother was the postmistress. Iris Aldous, twenty-nine years behind the counter, and in that last year she sorted the compensation letters herself, walked them up the lanes herself, because — she said — if the news was going to come, it would come with somebody who’d look you in the eye and stay for the kettle. She kept the post office open until there was no post left to sort. The forge went, the inn went, the school closed at Whitsun with eleven pupils and a hymn nobody could finish. In September the men from the water board went door to door with clipboards, collecting keys like the last of the harvest.

They moved the graves that summer — or said they did; Gran would only ever say *they moved the stones* — and held the final service at St Brannoc’s with the bishop down from the cathedral and the pews full of people who hadn’t prayed in years and didn’t exactly pray now. What do you sing, the last Sunday? She told me they sang the harvest hymn, in October, out of season, because nobody could think of a hymn for handing a valley over. The bell was taken down the next morning and sold to a parish in another county. The clock they left, because a clock in a tower is a building’s business. It was still telling ten past four when the water climbed past the face of it.

Iris stayed nearly to the end. There is a photograph — Tam has it, framed, in the hall where you can’t not see it — of my grandmother on the post office step in her Sunday coat, the water already silvering the far end of the high street, her chin set like a fence post. She was rowed out on the last morning by a young engineer called Pole, who apologised, she said, the whole way, as if the lake were something he’d spilled. And in the middle of the new water she made him ship the oars, and she stood up in the boat — Pole begging her to sit — and took the post office key from the ribbon at her neck and dropped it over the side.

*Let them ask me for it now,* she said.

She lived another sixty-one years, in a council house above the dam with a view of the drowning, and she never once called it the reservoir. It was *the water*, as in: the water’s high this year. The water’s giving nothing back. And in dry summers, when the lake sank and the old field walls came up out of it like knuckles: *the water’s thinking about it.*

She died in February, in the week of the floods, which Tam says was Iris all over — going out on a full lake so nobody could look at anything of hers.

This is the first summer of my life that the water has fallen without her.

***

I left the city before six the next morning and drove west with the sun coming up in the mirrors, and the whole way I made the mistake I always make, which is to believe I remember the road. The road remembers itself. It narrows by degrees, sheds its markings, gathers hedges; by eight it had shrugged off the last of the roundabouts and was running between banks of meadowsweet with grass growing down the middle like a mane. The radio gave out at the county line. I let it go.

You smell the dam before you see it. Wet stone and deep water — a cold, mineral breath that finds the car vents and settles on the back of your tongue, the smell of a cellar the size of a parish. Then the trees open and there it is, that long grey wall bedded into the hills like a held breath, and behind it, where the guidebooks say *lake*, the thing my family has always called the water.

Except the water wasn’t there.

Or it was — shrunken, sullen, pushed back into the deepest channel of the valley like something cornered. Around it, where thirty feet of lake should have stood, lay a wide brown country I had never seen and had heard described my whole life: the drawdown, the drought line, the bed of it. Acres of cracked and silvering mud, terraced with the old field walls, and a heron standing in the middle of a drowned lane as if it had been appointed.

The families’ opening was Saturday; this was Thursday. But Tam knows the warden, and the warden — a patient man named Brennan with a laminated map and the air of someone guarding a museum that had surfaced overnight — walked me down as far as the rope, where the county’s confidence ran out and the mud began. The signs said DANGER: SOFT GROUND, and then, in smaller and somehow more official letters, NO SOUVENIRS.

“Folk take doorknobs,” Brennan said, mournful. “Gate latches. A man Tuesday had a milestone in his boot. A milestone.”

From the rope, the village lay open to the sky for the first time in sixty-two years.

I had expected — I don’t know what I had expected. Ruins, I suppose. Romance. What the water gives back is stranger than ruin: a village skinned. The houses are gone to their footings, but the footings are all there, room after room drawn on the valley floor in lines of dark stone like a plan inked by an enormous patient hand. The high street is still cobbled. The cobbles shine. A garden wall keeps its gate, and the gate keeps its hinges, and on the hinges there are flakes of green paint, sixty-two years drowned and greener than the hills. By the school there is a bicycle grown into a wall, fused there in rust like a fossil fern, and in what was somebody’s pantry a shelf of jars stands unbroken, lids bloomed with sediment, holding whatever they hold.

And above it all, out where the mud gives way to the last deep water, St Brannoc’s raises its spire — not a stump, not a relic, the whole grey needle of it clear of the surface for the first time in my lifetime, streaked white and green like something hauled up, with the weathervane still at the tip of it. Bent, Tam had warned me. It is bent, the whole apparatus tilted a few degrees off true, so that the iron cockerel seems to be leaning into a wind that stopped blowing in 1963.

The clock faces the shore. The hands still say ten past four. My grandmother told me that all my life, and all my life I nodded the way you nod at family weather, and now I stood at the rope with the mud ticking and drying all around me in the evening sun, reading the time off a drowned church, and my eyes went hot.

I found the post office by counting doorways up from the pump, the way she taught me on the tablecloth with the cruet set for landmarks. Fifth up on the left, past the milestone — the milestone; I’d have to tell Brennan — and there it was: the long footing, the two shallow steps worn in the middle like soap, the slot of the doorway. The lintel above it has fallen, and on the fallen stone you can still read, in ghosted paint, letters my grandmother washed down every Friday of her working life: OFFICE.

I was turning away when I saw the door.

Not fallen. Doors fall; sixty years of water unhinges everything; the valley floor is scattered with them, lying flat and black like dropped playing cards. This one stood in its frame at the back of the old sorting room, upright, ajar — a door with nothing left to close, open exactly the width of a person, with the low sun laying its shadow neatly up the mud.

And leading to it, printed sharp and wet and new across ground the county had not yet opened to a living soul: one line of footprints.

Going in. None coming out.

I ducked under the rope.
