**Mara:** Welcome back to Room Tone. I'm Mara Voss. Episode ten: The Typeface Detective. June Okafor can walk past a hardware store, glance at the ghost sign painted above it, and tell you the year it went up, the foundry that cut the letters, and usually who copied whom. She calls it reading the street.

**June:** Letters are witnesses. A grocer in 1934 didn't choose that squared-off G because of fashion. He chose it because one sign painter in town owned one specimen book. Find the book, and you can date half the high street.

**Mara:** What is the rarest thing you've ever identified?

**June:** A butcher's fascia hiding under four layers of paint. The owner was sure it said nothing at all.

**June:** We raked light across it at midnight, and a whole alphabet from a dead foundry came up like a photograph developing.

**Mara:** You keep saying dead foundries. What kills a type foundry?

**June:** Weight, usually. Metal type is heavy and cities got expensive. When a foundry closed, its matrices were sold for scrap brass, and whole alphabets went into the melt. What survives is the printing — and the signs. A ghost sign can be the only specimen of a letterform left anywhere in the world.

**Mara:** So the high street is an archive that fades a little every day.

**June:** Every summer bleaches it a little more. I photograph the same forty signs every January, same lens, same corner. It is a slow-motion rescue, and we are losing, but we are losing slower than we were.

**Mara:** If a listener wants to start reading their own street, where do they look first?

**June:** Above the shopfronts, always — nobody renovates the second storey. Find the oldest G you can. The G is where a letterer shows off, and it will tell you more about your town than the plaque on the war memorial.
