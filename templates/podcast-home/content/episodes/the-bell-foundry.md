**Mara:** You're listening to Room Tone, a show about people who can't stop making things. I'm Mara Voss. Episode twelve: The Bell Foundry. This week I took the early train to a river town with one working foundry left in it. My guest is Tessa Marchetti, the third generation of her family to pour bronze into a mold and wait three days to hear what it says.

**Tessa:** Everyone thinks the pour is the dramatic part, and it is beautiful, all that orange light. But the pour is ten minutes. The waiting is the craft. A bell cools from the rim inward, and if you rush it, you lock a flaw into the voice of the thing forever.

**Mara:** So when do you first hear it? The moment the mold comes off?

**Tessa:** We don't strike it properly for a week. My grandfather used to tap the crown with his wedding ring, just once, and he could tell you the strike note and whether the hum was true.

**Tessa:** I once heard him reject a bell the town had already paid for. It sounded fine to me. It was not fine.

**Mara:** What was wrong with it?

**Tessa:** The hum note was eleven cents sharp against the strike. You would never hear it in isolation. But a bell is a chord, not a note — the strike, the hum an octave under it, the tierce singing a minor third above. If they disagree, the bell sounds anxious. People in the town would have felt it every Sunday without knowing why.

**Mara:** So he melted it down?

**Tessa:** He melted it down, ate the cost, and poured again with a corrected profile. That bell is still up there. When it rings for weddings, I hear my grandfather being stubborn.

**Mara:** You tune bells on a lathe now. Has the machine replaced the wedding ring?

**Tessa:** The lathe removes metal. It does not tell you when to stop. The ring still tells you when to stop.

**Mara:** How long will the newest bell you've poured outlive us?

**Tessa:** We guarantee a bell for a century, which embarrasses me to say out loud, because the one over our door is from 1741. Bronze is patient. You are only ever the middle generation.
